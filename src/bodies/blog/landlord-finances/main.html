Landlord Financial Management

Rental Property Finances in New Jersey:
A Landlord's Guide to Accounting and Tax

By Dan Rivera · Updated July 2026

Understanding the financial side of owning NJ rentals — tax obligations, cash flow math, and how to measure whether your investment is performing.

Open a separate bank account first

Run every rental transaction — rent in, repairs out — through a dedicated checking account, even if you own the property in your own name. Mixing rental and personal money makes tax preparation slower and more expensive, muddies your view of whether the property actually makes money, and, if you hold the property in an LLC, can undermine the liability separation the LLC exists to provide.

Track income and expenses per property

Treat each property — ideally each unit — as its own profit center, with categories that mirror IRS Schedule E: rents received, advertising, cleaning and maintenance, insurance, management fees, mortgage interest, repairs, taxes, utilities. When your bookkeeping categories match the tax form, year-end preparation becomes an export instead of a reconstruction. A spreadsheet is workable at 1-2 units; purpose-built software earns its subscription beyond that.

What is generally deductible

  • Repairs — fixing a leak, patching a wall — are typically deductible in the year paid. Improvements — a new roof, a renovated kitchen — generally must be depreciated over a period of years instead.
  • Depreciation — the building itself (not the land) is generally depreciated over 27.5 years for residential rentals. It is a significant non-cash deduction that first-year landlords often miss.
  • Mortgage interest, property taxes, and insurance premiums on the rental.
  • Management and leasing fees — see our pricing page for typical figures — along with legal and accounting costs.

The repair-versus-improvement line is genuinely gray, and rules change. Confirm your specific situation with a CPA who handles rental real estate.

Security deposits are not income

A security deposit is the tenant's money that you hold in trust. In New Jersey it must be kept in an interest-bearing account at a New Jersey banking institution, with written notice to the tenant, and it should never pass through your operating account unless you lawfully retain part of it at move-out. The full requirements are in our security deposit rules guide.

Collect W-9s and send contractor 1099s

If you pay an unincorporated contractor above the IRS reporting threshold in a calendar year, you generally need to issue Form 1099-NEC by January 31. The threshold is $2,000 for payments made in 2026, raised from the long-standing $600 — confirm the current figure and whether it applies to your rental activity with your CPA. The practical habit: collect a signed W-9 before a contractor's first payment. Chasing one the following January is much harder.

The numbers to watch monthly

Beyond raw bookkeeping, three figures tell you whether a property is healthy. Collection rate: rent actually received divided by rent billed — anything under 100% for two consecutive months deserves a conversation, not a wait-and-see. Maintenance cost per unit, tracked over a trailing 12 months so single big repairs do not distort the picture. Net operating income: income minus operating expenses, before the mortgage — the number a future buyer or lender will price the property on. Reviewing these three takes 15 minutes a month and catches most problems while they are still small.

Monthly statements, one year-end package

Whether you self-manage or hire it out, produce a monthly statement per property: rent collected, expenses paid, net to owner. At year end you want a single package — income statement, expense detail with receipts, mortgage interest statement, and any 1099s — that goes straight to your tax preparer. Professional management typically includes this reporting; our guide to management costs covers what the fee buys.

Keep real cash reserves

A common rule of thumb is 3-6 months of operating expenses per unit — at minimum, enough to absorb a furnace replacement and a month of vacancy in the same quarter. For ongoing upkeep on New Jersey's older housing stock, budgeting roughly one month of rent per unit per year for maintenance and turnover costs is a reasonable starting point. Actual spending is lumpy, which is exactly why the reserve exists.
