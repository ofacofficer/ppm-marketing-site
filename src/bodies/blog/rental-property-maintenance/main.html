Rental Property Maintenance

Rental Property Maintenance in NJ:
What Landlords Are Responsible For

By Dan Rivera · Updated July 2026

Maintenance is where most landlord-tenant disputes start. Understanding your obligations — and staying ahead of them — is how you avoid costly problems.

The legal floor: habitability

Every residential lease in New Jersey carries an implied warranty of habitability — an ongoing duty to keep the unit safe and livable regardless of what the lease says. The doctrine comes from Marini v. Ireland (1970), the case that also established a tenant's right to repair and deduct: if a landlord fails to fix a vital defect after notice, the tenant may hire the repair done and subtract the cost from rent. Heat, hot water, working plumbing and electricity, a sound roof, and freedom from pests are the floor. Everything below is about staying comfortably above it.

Run a preventive calendar

WhenWhat
SpringClean gutters, inspect roof and exterior after winter, service AC before the first heat wave
FallService the furnace or boiler, clean gutters after leaf drop, check weatherstripping and window seals
AnnuallyFlush the water heater, test GFCI outlets, inspect caulk and grout in wet areas
Every turnoverSmoke alarm, CO alarm, and fire extinguisher certificate (required in NJ at each change of occupancy), plus a full photo walkthrough

Preventive work is cheap next to what it prevents: a $150-250 furnace service against a mid-January no-heat emergency, or an afternoon of gutter cleaning against water in a finished basement.

Triage every request

Emergencies — no heat in winter, an active leak, gas odor, no running water, sewage backup, exposed wiring — need same-day action, and several of them are habitability problems from hour one. Urgent items — a dead refrigerator, the only shower not working — merit a fix within 24-48 hours. Routine items — a dripping faucet, a torn screen — can be scheduled within a week. Tell tenants at lease signing how to report each category and what response time to expect; a large share of maintenance disputes are really communication disputes.

A vendor bench beats one handyman

The single-handyman model fails at the worst possible time — when he is unreachable during a February no-heat call. Build working relationships with at least five trades: a licensed plumber, a licensed electrician, an HVAC contractor, a general handyman, and a locksmith. Licensed trades cost more per visit and are required for permit work in NJ; an unlicensed repair that later causes damage can also create insurance problems.

Document everything

Keep a written record of every request: date reported, what was reported, when you responded, who did the work, what it cost, and when it was completed — with photos before and after. Move-in and move-out photos protect the security deposit process, and the maintenance log is your evidence if a tenant later claims neglect.

New Jersey winter items

Winterize before Thanksgiving: shut off and drain exterior spigots, insulate pipes in unheated spaces, and confirm the heating system holds temperature under load. Ice dams — meltwater refreezing at the roof edge — cause interior leaks that get misdiagnosed as roof failure; attic insulation and clear gutters are the prevention. In vacant units, keep the thermostat at 55°F or higher. A burst pipe in an empty apartment is among the most expensive claims a landlord can file.

When neglect becomes legal exposure

Deferred maintenance converts into legal risk along a predictable path: the tenant reports a problem, nothing happens, and the tenant then repairs and deducts, seeks a rent abatement, or calls the municipal code office — all remedies New Jersey law supports. Code violations carry fines, and an injury traced to a known, unfixed defect brings liability claims. The pattern in the case law is consistent: landlords lose when they knew about the defect and did not act. A documented, responsive process is both the operational fix and the legal defense.
