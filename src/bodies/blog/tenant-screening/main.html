Tenant Screening in NJ

How to Screen Tenants in New Jersey:
A Landlord's Complete Guide

By Dan Rivera · Updated July 2026

Finding the right tenant is the single most important decision in managing a rental property. Here's how to do it properly in New Jersey.

Start with written screening criteria

Before the first showing, write down the standards every applicant must meet and apply them in the order complete applications arrive. A typical set for an NJ rental: gross monthly income of at least 3x the tenant's share of the rent, no eviction filings in the past 5 years, a credit history that shows bills paid on time, and positive references from prior landlords. Written criteria do two jobs. They keep your judgment consistent when an applicant makes a strong personal impression, and they are your best evidence that you treated everyone the same if a fair housing complaint is ever filed.

Verify income, then pull credit the legal way

An income standard of about three times the rent is a common benchmark, but since January 12, 2026 New Jersey law requires any income standard to be based only on the portion of the rent the tenant pays (P.L.2025, c.251, amending N.J.S.A. 10:5-12). When a voucher or other assistance covers part of the rent, measure income against the tenant's share alone. Verify it with 2-4 recent pay stubs, an offer letter for new hires, or two years of tax returns for self-employed applicants — and confirm employment through the employer's main line, not a phone number the applicant supplies.

Run credit and eviction history through a screening service that follows the federal Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA). You need the applicant's written consent, and if you deny an application — or approve it with conditions such as a co-signer or a larger deposit — based in whole or in part on the report, you must send an adverse-action notice that names the reporting agency and tells the applicant they can request a free copy of the report.

Criminal history: NJ's Fair Chance in Housing Act

Since January 2022, New Jersey landlords may not ask about criminal history on the initial application or run a criminal background check before making a conditional offer. After a conditional offer, you may consider only certain convictions within lookback windows set by the law, and you must complete an individualized assessment — the nature of the offense, how long ago it occurred, evidence of rehabilitation — before withdrawing the offer in writing with the reason stated. Civil penalties start at up to $1,000 for a first violation and reach $10,000 for repeat violations. The one check permitted at any stage is the lifetime sex-offender registry.

Fair housing: federal and state layers

The federal Fair Housing Act bars discrimination based on race, color, religion, national origin, sex, disability, and familial status. New Jersey's Law Against Discrimination adds more protected categories, including marital status, sexual orientation, gender identity, and lawful source of income. That last one matters in daily practice: you cannot refuse an applicant because they hold a Section 8 voucher or other rental assistance, and income standards should be applied to the portion of the rent the tenant actually pays. Call employers and past landlords

Call the current landlord and at least one prior landlord. The prior landlord is often the more candid reference — a current landlord dealing with a problem tenant has an incentive to help that tenant move on. Ask specific questions: Did they pay on time? How did they leave the unit? Would you rent to them again?

Red flags worth slowing down for

  • Offers of several months' rent up front in exchange for skipping screening
  • Landlord references that trace back to friends or relatives — check the property's owner in municipal tax records
  • Pay stubs that do not match what the employer confirms
  • Inconsistencies between the application and the credit report: addresses, employers, name variations
  • Pressure to sign quickly or move in before screening is complete

None of these automatically fails your written criteria, but each justifies verification before you hand over keys.

Document every decision

Keep applications, screening reports, reference notes, and copies of adverse-action letters for every applicant — approved and denied — for at least 2 years. If a discrimination complaint arrives, the file showing that the same criteria produced every decision is your defense.
