How to Become a Landlord in New Jersey: Everything You Need to Know First

By Dan Rivera, updated July 2026

Renting out a property in New Jersey for the first time comes with more complexity than most people expect. Here's what to know before you hand over the keys.

1. Register the property

Every residential rental in New Jersey must be registered. Buildings with one or two units file a landlord registration statement with the municipal clerk. Buildings with three or more units register instead with the state Department of Community Affairs (DCA), which also inspects them on a roughly 5-year cycle. This is not optional paperwork — a court can hold up an eviction case until the registration is on file.

2. Pass the turnover inspections

Most NJ municipalities require a certificate of occupancy or re-rental habitability inspection before each new tenancy. Separately, state fire code requires a certificate confirming working smoke alarms, carbon monoxide alarms, and a portable fire extinguisher at every change of occupancy. Fees, scheduling, and lead-based paint inspection requirements vary town by town, so call your municipal office before you list the unit.

3. Carry liability insurance and file the certificate

A 2022 state law requires rental owners to carry liability coverage of at least $500,000 — or $300,000 for owner-occupied buildings of four units or fewer — and to register the certificate of insurance with the municipality annually. Note that a standard homeowner's policy does not cover a rental; you need a landlord (dwelling fire) policy.

4. Use an NJ-specific lease and handle the deposit correctly

Generic internet leases routinely include clauses that are unenforceable in New Jersey. Use a lease written for NJ law, and treat the security deposit as regulated money: the maximum is 1.5 months' rent, it must sit in an interest-bearing account at a New Jersey banking institution, and the tenant must receive written notice of where it is held within 30 days. The full rules — including the 30-day return deadline and the double-damages penalty for getting it wrong — are in our security deposit guide.

5. Check rent control before setting the rent

Roughly 100 New Jersey municipalities have rent control ordinances, each with its own increase cap, exemptions, and vacancy rules. Whether your unit is covered depends on the town, the building size, and sometimes the building's age. Check your municipality in our rent control lookup before you advertise a number.

6. Price from comps, not from your mortgage

Tenants pay market rent, not your carrying costs. Pull 5-10 comparable active and recently rented listings within about a mile — same bedroom count, similar condition — and price inside that range. A unit priced $100 over market that sits vacant an extra six weeks costs more than the higher rent would have earned over the whole year.

7. Decide how to hold title

Many first-time landlords hold the property personally and rely on insurance; others form an LLC to separate the property's liabilities from personal assets. An LLC adds costs — formation, annual filings, sometimes lender consent or different loan terms — and it does not replace insurance. The right structure depends on your finances and your lender, so talk to a New Jersey attorney and a CPA before closing. Moving a mortgaged property into an LLC after the fact can create complications.

The first 90 days, in order

  1. Confirm registration, inspection, and insurance requirements with your municipality
  2. Finish repairs and safety items before photos and showings
  3. Set the rent from comps and any rent control ordinance
  4. Screen every applicant against the same written criteria
  5. Sign an NJ lease, deposit the security deposit correctly, and document the unit's condition with dated photos
