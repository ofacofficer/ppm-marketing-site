NJ Eviction Process Timeline: Step-by-Step Guide for Landlords (2026)

By Proactive Property Management · Published July 2026
By Proactive Property Management · July 29, 2026 · Proactive Property Management

Legal Disclaimer: This article is general information only, not legal advice. NJ eviction law is technical and fact-specific. Consult a licensed New Jersey attorney before taking any action.

If a tenant stops paying rent or violates their lease, New Jersey law gives you a clear — but strict — process to follow. Skip a step, use the wrong notice, or try a shortcut and you risk starting over from square one. This guide walks through every stage of the NJ eviction process, with realistic timelines so you know what to expect.

Overview: How Long Does a NJ Eviction Take?

A straightforward non-payment eviction in New Jersey typically takes 30 to 90 days from the first notice to the lockout — assuming no tenant defenses, no adjournments, and prompt landlord action at each step. Lease-violation or holdover evictions can run longer because the required notice periods are extended. Contested cases with tenant attorney representation can stretch to four to six months or more.

The table below summarizes each step and its typical duration:

Step Action Typical Duration
1 Confirm lawful grounds for eviction 1–3 days
2 Serve correct written notice 3 days – 3 months (notice period)
3 File Complaint in Special Civil Part 1–5 days to file; hearing scheduled 10–30 days out
4 Court hearing Day of; adjournments add 1–4 weeks each
5 Judgment for Possession issued Same day as hearing (if uncontested)
6 Warrant for Removal issued & served 3–10 days after judgment
7 Lockout with Special Civil Part officer After 3-day statutory notice on warrant

Note: Timelines are estimates based on general NJ court practice and may vary by county. Verify current court scheduling with the NJ Superior Court or a licensed NJ attorney before relying on any specific timeframe.

Step 1 — Grounds for Eviction (N.J.S.A. 2A:18-61.1)

New Jersey is a "just cause" eviction state. Under N.J.S.A. 2A:18-61.1, a landlord may only evict a residential tenant for one of the specific statutory grounds listed in that section. You cannot evict simply because you want the unit back. Common grounds include:

  • Nonpayment of rent — tenant fails to pay rent due
  • Disorderly conduct — tenant or occupants cause a nuisance
  • Willful destruction of property — tenant damages the unit
  • Violation of a lease covenant — tenant breaches a written lease term after written notice
  • Habitual late payment — tenant persistently pays rent after the due date
  • Holdover after lease expiration — tenant remains after the lease term ends and proper notice is given
  • Conviction of a drug offense on the premises
  • Owner occupancy — owner or immediate family intends to occupy (specific notice requirements apply)

This list is illustrative, not exhaustive. The full statutory grounds are enumerated in N.J.S.A. 2A:18-61.1. Confirm the applicable ground with a licensed NJ attorney before proceeding. This is general information, not legal advice.

Identifying the correct ground matters because it determines which type of notice you must serve in Step 2.

Step 2 — Serve the Correct Notice

Before filing with the court, you must serve a written notice on the tenant. The required notice period depends on the ground for eviction. Using the wrong notice — or serving it incorrectly — is one of the most common reasons NJ eviction cases are dismissed.

  • 3-Day Notice to Quit (Nonpayment of Rent) — For failure to pay rent, you serve a written demand giving the tenant at least three days to pay the full amount owed or vacate. If the tenant pays in full within three days, the eviction notice is generally cured.
  • 1-Month Notice to Quit (Holdover / Month-to-Month Tenancy) — For a month-to-month tenant you do not wish to renew, a full calendar month's written notice is typically required.
  • 3-Month Notice (Certain Lease Violations) — For certain lease covenant violations, NJ law requires a longer cure period before you can proceed to court. Specific durations vary by violation type.
  • Lease-violation cure notice — For many lease violations, the notice must specify the breach and give the tenant an opportunity to cure before the eviction complaint can be filed.

How to serve the notice: Notices must generally be delivered to the tenant personally, left with a person of suitable age and discretion at the premises, or — if no one is home — affixed to the main entrance door. Keep dated proof of service. Do not rely solely on email or text message without confirming it meets NJ service rules.

Notice period requirements are governed by N.J.S.A. 2A:18-61.1 et seq. and applicable case law. Always verify the correct notice type and period with a licensed NJ attorney before serving. This is general information, not legal advice.

Step 3 — File a Complaint in Special Civil Part

Once the notice period expires without cure, you may file an eviction complaint (Landlord-Tenant Complaint) in the Special Civil Part of the NJ Superior Court in the county where the property is located. Key points:

  • Use the official Landlord-Tenant Complaint form available at the NJ Courts website (njcourts.gov) or the courthouse clerk's office.
  • Filing fees apply; the exact amount is set by the NJ Judiciary and subject to change — confirm the current fee schedule with the clerk at your county courthouse.
  • The court will schedule a hearing date, typically 10 to 30 days after filing, depending on county court volume.
  • The court (not the landlord) serves the tenant with the complaint and hearing notice by certified and regular mail, and in some cases by personal service through the Special Civil Part officer.
  • If you are a corporate landlord (LLC, corporation), NJ court rules generally require you to be represented by a licensed attorney. Individual landlords may represent themselves (pro se).

Court rules and forms change. Confirm current requirements directly with the NJ Superior Court Special Civil Part or a licensed NJ attorney before filing. This is general information, not legal advice.

Step 4 — Court Hearing: What to Bring

On the hearing date, both landlord and tenant appear before a judge or hearing officer in Special Civil Part. Come prepared:

  • Signed copy of the lease agreement
  • Rent ledger showing the amount owed and payment history
  • Proof of service of the eviction notice (affidavit, certified mail receipt, etc.)
  • Any written communications with the tenant relevant to the ground for eviction
  • Photo or written evidence if the claim involves property damage or lease violation

Adjournments: Tenants may request more time; judges have discretion to grant adjournments. Each adjournment typically adds one to four weeks to the timeline. In cases involving tenants seeking emergency rental assistance, courts may grant additional time under applicable law.

Settlement: Many NJ eviction cases settle at the courthouse through a consent order — for example, the tenant agrees to pay arrears by a specific date in exchange for the landlord withdrawing the complaint. Understand your options before accepting or rejecting any settlement.

Step 5 — Judgment for Possession and Warrant for Removal

If the judge rules in your favor, the court issues a Judgment for Possession. This is not yet a lockout — it is the court's ruling that you are entitled to regain possession of the property.

After the judgment, you request a Warrant for Removal from the court (typically through the clerk immediately following the hearing). The Special Civil Part officer then serves the warrant on the tenant. Under NJ court rules, the warrant must give the tenant a minimum statutory period — generally three days — to vacate voluntarily before the officer executes the lockout.

Tenant's right to pay and stay (nonpayment cases): In a nonpayment eviction, a tenant may have a statutory right under NJ law to pay the full amount of rent owed (including court costs) at or before the time the warrant is executed to stop the eviction. This is commonly called the "pay and stay" right. The scope and timing rules are governed by statute and should be confirmed with a licensed NJ attorney.

Judgment and warrant procedures are governed by NJ Court Rules and N.J.S.A. 2A:18 et seq. Confirm current rules with a licensed NJ attorney. This is general information, not legal advice.

Step 6 — Lockout and What Landlords Cannot Do

The actual lockout is carried out by a Special Civil Part officer (a court officer) — not by the landlord. The officer arrives at the property, confirms the tenant's identity, and supervises the removal.

What landlords CANNOT do — self-help eviction is illegal in NJ:

  • ❌ Change the locks without a court warrant and officer present
  • ❌ Remove the tenant's belongings without the officer
  • ❌ Shut off utilities (heat, water, electricity) to force a tenant out
  • ❌ Remove doors or windows
  • ❌ Threaten, harass, or intimidate a tenant to leave
  • ❌ Enter and remove a tenant's property without authorization

Self-help eviction in New Jersey exposes the landlord to civil liability, potential criminal charges, and may result in the tenant being restored to possession by the court. There are no shortcuts — the warrant process exists to protect both parties.

After the lockout: Once the tenant has vacated, you may change the locks. Any personal property left behind by the tenant is subject to NJ's abandoned property rules — consult an attorney before disposing of anything of value.

Self-help eviction prohibitions are grounded in NJ common law and statute. This is general information, not legal advice. Consult a licensed NJ attorney.

How Professional Property Management Reduces Eviction Timeline Risk

Most eviction delays in New Jersey are avoidable — wrong notice type, missing documentation, improper service, or missed court dates. A professional property manager reduces those risks at every stage:

  • Lease drafting: A tight, NJ-compliant lease makes the grounds for eviction clear from day one and reduces tenant defenses.
  • Rent tracking and early intervention: Identifying non-payment on day 1 — not day 30 — means notices go out before arrears compound.
  • Correct notice, correctly served: Managers who handle NJ evictions regularly know which notice type applies and how to document service in a way that holds up in court.
  • Court-ready documentation: Organized rent ledgers, signed lease, and dated communications — assembled before the hearing date, not scrambled together the morning of.
  • Coordination with eviction counsel: A manager who has existing relationships with NJ landlord-tenant attorneys can connect you faster when the matter needs legal representation.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does the NJ eviction process take?

A straightforward nonpayment eviction in New Jersey typically takes 30 to 90 days from the initial notice to the court-supervised lockout. That range assumes no tenant defenses, no adjournments, and that the landlord files promptly after the notice period expires. Contested cases with attorney representation, adjournments, or cases involving emergency rental assistance can take four to six months or longer. County court volume also affects scheduling.

Can I change the locks on a tenant in NJ?

No — not until a court-ordered lockout is executed by a Special Civil Part officer. Changing the locks on a tenant before that point is illegal self-help eviction in New Jersey and exposes the landlord to civil liability and potential court sanctions. The only lawful way to remove a tenant is through the court process ending with the warrant for removal and officer-supervised lockout.

What is a notice to quit in NJ?

A notice to quit is the written notice a landlord must serve on a tenant before filing an eviction complaint in court. It informs the tenant of the reason for the eviction and gives them a statutory period — as short as three days for nonpayment, or as long as three months for certain lease violations — to either pay, cure the violation, or vacate. Serving the correct type of notice is a legal prerequisite; filing without a proper notice typically results in dismissal.

Can a tenant stop an eviction in NJ by paying rent at the last minute?

In nonpayment eviction cases, New Jersey law gives tenants significant rights to pay arrears and halt an eviction — even at or near the time a warrant for removal is to be executed. This is commonly called the "pay and stay" right. The exact rules around timing and how many times a tenant can exercise this right are governed by statute and case law. Landlords should understand these rules and discuss them with a licensed NJ attorney to set realistic expectations.

What is the Special Civil Part in NJ?

The Special Civil Part is a division of the NJ Superior Court that handles landlord-tenant matters, including all residential eviction complaints. It is located in the courthouse of each county. Landlords file their eviction complaints there, hearings are held there, and the Special Civil Part officers execute warrants for removal. Filing must be done in the county where the rental property is located.

Do I need a lawyer to evict a tenant in NJ?

Individual landlords may represent themselves (pro se) in NJ Special Civil Part eviction proceedings. However, if the landlord is a corporation or LLC, NJ court rules generally require representation by a licensed attorney. Even for individual landlords, an attorney is strongly recommended for contested cases, tenants with legal representation, or cases involving complex grounds. The cost of getting it wrong — a dismissal and restarting the process — almost always exceeds the cost of counsel.

Can a landlord evict a tenant for no reason in NJ?

No. New Jersey is a "just cause" eviction state under N.J.S.A. 2A:18-61.1. A landlord must have a specific statutory ground — such as nonpayment of rent, lease violation, disorderly conduct, or holdover after proper notice — to file an eviction complaint. A landlord cannot evict a tenant simply because the lease has expired or because they want the unit back for a non-statutory reason. This applies to virtually all residential tenants in NJ.
