How Long Does the NJ Eviction Process Take? (Step-by-Step Timeline)

By Proactive Property Management · Published September 2026
By Proactive Property Management · September 3, 2026 · Proactive Property Management

Most New Jersey landlords facing their first eviction make the same mistake: they assume the process takes a few weeks. In reality, even an uncontested case can stretch two to three months from first notice to lockout — and contested cases routinely run longer. Understanding each step, and what can extend it, is the difference between a manageable situation and a cash-flow crisis.

This guide walks through every required stage of the New Jersey eviction process in sequence, with realistic time estimates at each step. Note: statute citations throughout this article are flagged for attorney confirmation before publication; nothing here constitutes legal advice.

Step 1 — Written Notice to Quit

Before filing anything in court, New Jersey law requires a landlord to serve the tenant with a written Notice to Quit (sometimes called a Notice to Cease or Notice to Vacate, depending on the ground). The required notice period varies by the reason for eviction under N.J.S.A. 2A:18-61.1 (attorney review pending):

  • Nonpayment of rent — No prior written notice is technically required before filing; however, landlords typically send a demand letter as a practical first step and to document the record.
  • Habitual late payment — A Notice to Cease is required first, then a Notice to Quit giving the tenant one month to vacate.
  • Lease violation (other than nonpayment) — A Notice to Cease is required; if the violation continues, a Notice to Quit giving one month to vacate follows.
  • Disorderly conduct / damage to property — Three days' notice to quit.
  • Drug-related crime on the premises — Three days' notice to quit.
  • End of lease / no-cause (where permitted) — One month's notice for month-to-month tenancies; longer for annual leases. Note that NJ's Anti-Eviction Act limits no-cause terminations significantly — confirm with counsel.

All notice periods above are subject to attorney confirmation against current statute and any applicable local ordinance.

Time at this stage: 3 days to 1 month+, depending on ground.

Step 2 — Filing a Complaint in NJ Superior Court, Special Civil Part

Once the notice period has run and the tenant has not vacated or cured, the landlord files a Complaint for Possession in the Special Civil Part of the New Jersey Superior Court in the county where the property is located. This is a summary (expedited) dispossess proceeding — it is not a general civil lawsuit.

What landlords need to bring or include:

  • A completed Complaint for Possession form (available at the courthouse or the NJ Courts website)
  • A copy of the lease (if written) and any addenda
  • Proof of notice served (certified mail receipt, process server affidavit, or personal service documentation)
  • Filing fee — confirm current fee with the Special Civil Part clerk or the NJ Courts fee schedule; fees are subject to change

After filing, the court schedules a hearing date. In most counties, hearings are set 10–30 days from the filing date, though this varies by county docket volume. The tenant is served with a summons notifying them of the court date.

Time at this stage: 2–5 weeks (filing through hearing date).

Step 3 — Court Hearing Date & Judgment for Possession

On the hearing date, both landlord and tenant appear before a judge or hearing officer in the Special Civil Part. What happens depends on the tenant's response:

  • Tenant does not appear — The court typically enters a default judgment for possession in the landlord's favor.
  • Tenant appears and does not contest — Judgment for possession is usually entered; the court may set a move-out date or a "pay and stay" amount for nonpayment cases.
  • Tenant appears and contests — The case may be heard the same day or adjourned to a later date. Contested cases can add 2–6 additional weeks to the timeline, sometimes more.
  • Tenant raises a habitability defense — If the tenant claims landlord breach (e.g., uninhabitable conditions), the court may conduct a more involved hearing or order an inspection, adding further time.

If the landlord prevails, the court enters a Judgment for Possession. Tenants generally have a short period (often 3 days, confirm with counsel) to file a motion to stay or appeal before the warrant stage begins.

Time at this stage: same-day (uncontested) to 4–8 weeks (contested).

Step 4 — Warrant for Removal & Lockout

After judgment, the landlord requests a Warrant for Removal from the court clerk. This is the document that authorizes the actual physical eviction. Key points:

  • The warrant is typically issued 3 business days after judgment in nonpayment cases, giving the tenant a final window to pay in full and reinstate the tenancy (attorney review pending on current cure period).
  • Once issued, the warrant is served by a court-appointed officer (a Special Civil Part Officer, not a sheriff in most NJ counties). The officer posts the warrant on the property and schedules a lockout date — typically within 3–7 days of service.
  • On lockout day, the officer supervises removal of the tenant and the landlord (or their agent) re-takes possession and changes the locks.
  • Tenants may file an Order to Show Cause to stay the lockout; courts sometimes grant a brief stay (days to a couple of weeks) on a showing of hardship or procedural error.

Time at this stage: 1–3 weeks from judgment to lockout (barring a stay).

How Long Total? Realistic Ranges

Adding the stages together, here is what most NJ landlords actually experience:

  • Best case (nonpayment, uncontested, tenant vacates after judgment): 5–8 weeks from first notice to possession
  • Typical uncontested case: 8–12 weeks
  • Contested case (tenant appears, raises defenses, no immediate adjournment): 3–5 months
  • Heavily contested case (multiple adjournments, stays, appeals): 6 months or longer

These are estimates only; county docket load, tenant conduct, and case-specific facts all affect the actual timeline.

What Can Slow It Down

Even a well-prepared landlord can face delays. The most common ones:

  • Defective notice — Wrong notice period, wrong form, or improper service voids the notice and restarts the clock.
  • Service problems — Tenant avoidance of service requires re-service or substituted service, adding days or weeks.
  • County docket volume — Some counties schedule hearings weeks out; urban counties in particular can run slower.
  • Adjournments — Either party may request a continuance; courts often grant at least one.
  • Tenant bankruptcy filing — An automatic stay under federal bankruptcy law can pause eviction proceedings, sometimes significantly.
  • Order to Show Cause — Tenant files an emergency motion to stay the warrant; even if denied, it adds time.
  • Uninhabitable-conditions defense — If the property has open habitability issues, the court may hold the eviction and order repairs first.
  • Local eviction moratoriums — Confirm whether any municipal or state moratorium currently applies; this area of law can change (attorney review required).

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does an eviction take in New Jersey?

A straightforward, uncontested nonpayment case typically takes 8–12 weeks from the initial notice to the lockout. Contested cases routinely run 3–5 months or longer, depending on county docket load, tenant defenses, and whether a stay is granted.

Can a landlord evict a tenant without going to court in NJ?

No. New Jersey law requires a court judgment for possession before a landlord may remove a tenant. Self-help eviction — changing locks, removing belongings, or shutting off utilities to force a tenant out — is illegal and exposes the landlord to significant liability.

What notice is required before filing for eviction in NJ?

It depends on the reason. Nonpayment of rent can go to court without prior written notice in some circumstances, though a demand letter is standard practice. Lease violations require a Notice to Cease followed by a Notice to Quit. Disorderly conduct and drug-related grounds require three days' notice. Always confirm notice requirements with a licensed NJ attorney before proceeding.

What court handles evictions in New Jersey?

Evictions (dispossess proceedings) are filed in the Special Civil Part of the New Jersey Superior Court in the county where the rental property is located. This is a summary proceeding designed to move faster than general civil litigation.

How long after judgment can a tenant stay in New Jersey?

After a Judgment for Possession is entered, the landlord may apply for a Warrant for Removal. In nonpayment cases, the tenant generally has a brief window (often around three days — confirm with counsel) to pay the full amount owed and avoid removal. Once the warrant is served, the lockout is typically scheduled within days.

Can a property manager handle an eviction for me in New Jersey?

A property manager coordinates the process — monitoring payments, issuing compliant notices, coordinating with your eviction attorney, and managing the lockout. The actual court filings and appearances require a licensed NJ attorney (landlords may represent themselves, but it is rarely advisable).
